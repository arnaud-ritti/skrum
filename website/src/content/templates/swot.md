## What it is

SWOT sorts what the team knows about its situation into four columns, along two lines: inside or outside the team, helpful or harmful. Strengths and weaknesses belong to the team. Opportunities and threats come from around it: the organisation, users, the market.

## Goal

A shared picture of where the team stands, and a short list of moves that follow from it: a strength to use, a weakness to fix, an opening to take, a risk to prepare for.

## When to use it

- At a turning point rather than at the end of an ordinary sprint: the start of a quarter or a project, after a reorganisation, when a team forms or takes over a product.
- When the retrospectives keep returning to the same local problems and the team needs to look further out.

## When to pick another format

SWOT is too wide for looking back at one sprint: none of its columns asks what happened. Take [Went well, To improve, Action ideas](../went-well-to-improve-actions/) or [Starfish](../starfish/). If the team is discouraged and a list of weaknesses and threats would weigh on it, [SOAR Analysis](../soar/) starts from the same strengths and opportunities and goes on to what the team wants. To find the cause of one problem, use [Fishbone Analysis](../fishbone/).

## How to run it

Name the subject first: the team, a product, a project. Without it, cards land at different scales.

1. **Strengths**: what can we rely on that is ours?
2. **Weaknesses**: what, inside the team, costs us time or quality?
3. **Opportunities**: what is opening around us that we could take while it is open?
4. **Threats**: what outside the team would hurt us if nothing changes?

Cards often land in the wrong half, a weakness written as a threat. When grouping, ask of each card whether it is the team's own or comes from outside, and move it. Then read across the columns: which strength helps take an opportunity, which weakness makes a threat worse. Those pairings are usually where the actions are. Vote, and give each chosen card an action with an owner.
